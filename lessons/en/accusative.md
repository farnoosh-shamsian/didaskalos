# The Accusative Case

The **accusative** is the case of the goal. Its central job is to name the thing an action is done *to* — the direct object — but Greek extends that idea a long way: the accusative also measures how far and how long, names the respect in which something is true, carries the subject of an infinitive, and follows the great majority of prepositions.

It is one of the easiest cases to recognize: outside the neuter, an accusative almost always ends in **‑ν** in the singular and **‑ς** in the plural.

---

## 1. Direct Object

The noun that receives the action of a transitive verb stands in the accusative.

- **τὸν νόμον λέγει.** — "He states the law."
- **τοὺς φίλους ὁρῶμεν.** — "We see our friends."

Everything agreeing with that noun is accusative too — its article, its adjectives, a participle describing it: **τοὺς ἀγαθοὺς πολίτας τιμῶμεν** "we honour the good citizens."

Greek and English do not agree about which verbs are transitive. Several very common Greek verbs take a genitive or a dative where English has a plain object (ἀκούω "hear" + gen. of the person, πείθομαι "obey" + dat.), and a few take an accusative where English needs a preposition (φεύγω τὴν δίκην "be defendant *in* a suit"). Learn the case along with the verb.

---

## 2. Double Accusative

Verbs of teaching, asking, dressing, depriving and naming can govern **two** accusatives at once — one of the person, one of the thing.

- **διδάσκω τοὺς παῖδας τὴν μουσικήν.** — "I teach the children music."
- **ταῦτα ὑμᾶς ἐρωτῶ.** — "I ask you this."

A second family takes an object plus a **predicate** accusative, naming what the object is made or called: **στρατηγὸν αὐτὸν εἵλοντο** "they chose him general."

---

## 3. Subject of the Infinitive

When an infinitive has a subject of its own — different from the subject of the main verb — that subject stands in the accusative. This construction is everywhere in Attic prose after verbs of saying and thinking.

- **φησὶ τοὺς ἄνδρας ἥκειν.** — "He says that the men have come."
- **νομίζω τοῦτο δίκαιον εἶναι.** — "I consider this to be just."

Note the consequence: a predicate word inside such a clause is accusative as well (δίκαιον above), because it agrees with the accusative subject.

---

## 4. After Prepositions

Most prepositions govern the accusative, and several govern it alongside another case with a different meaning. The core sense is **motion towards** or **extension over**:

- **εἰς τὴν πόλιν** "into the city" · **πρὸς ὑμᾶς** "to you" · **ἐπὶ τοὺς πολεμίους** "against the enemy"
- **κατὰ τοὺς νόμους** "according to the laws" · **διὰ τοῦτο** "because of this" · **παρὰ τὸν νόμον** "contrary to the law"

The module *Prepositions* sets these out case by case; for now, expect an accusative after a preposition unless you have a reason not to.

---

## 5. Extent of Space and Time

The bare accusative — no preposition — measures **how far** and **how long**.

- **ἐνιαυτὸν ἔμεινεν.** — "He stayed for a year."
- **δέκα σταδίους ἐπορεύθησαν.** — "They marched ten stades."

Contrast the genitive ("within which") and the dative ("at which"): πολλῶν ἐτῶν "within many years", ταύτῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ "on that day", but πολὺν χρόνον "for a long time".

---

## 6. Accusative of Respect

The accusative can name the **part or aspect** in which a statement holds — often best rendered "in respect of", "as to".

- **πόδας ὠκύς** — "swift **of foot**."
- **ἀλγῶ τὴν κεφαλήν.** — "I have a pain **in my head**."

The neuter accusative of an adjective used adverbially belongs here too: **πολύ** "much, far", **οὐδέν** "not at all", **τί;** "why?", **πρῶτον** "first". These are extremely common and look like objects until you notice there is no verb to govern them.

---

## 7. Accusative in Oaths

After the oath particles **μά** (denial) and **νή** (affirmation) the god sworn by stands in the accusative.

- **μὰ Δία, οὐκ ἔγωγε.** — "By Zeus, not I!"
- **νὴ Δία, καλῶς γε.** — "By Zeus, quite right."

μά with no negative in sight is still a denial; the particle carries it. These little oaths are a normal feature of Athenian dialogue and oratory and turn up constantly in Plato and Demosthenes.

---

## 8. The Endings, Declension by Declension

Note the pattern that runs through all three declensions: **‑ν** in the masculine and feminine singular, **‑ς** in the masculine and feminine plural, and neuters identical to the nominative in both numbers.

### 1st Declension (‑ᾱ/‑ᾰ/‑η stems)

| Gender | Nominative | Accusative sg. | Accusative pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feminine (‑η) | ἡ τιμή | τὴν τιμήν | τὰς τιμάς |
| Feminine (‑ᾱ) | ἡ ἀγορά | τὴν ἀγοράν | τὰς ἀγοράς |
| Feminine (‑ᾰ) | ἡ θάλαττα | τὴν θάλατταν | τὰς θαλάττας |
| Masculine (‑ης) | ὁ πολίτης | τὸν πολίτην | τοὺς πολίτας |
| Masculine (‑ᾱς) | ὁ νεανίας | τὸν νεανίαν | τοὺς νεανίας |

The singular keeps the vowel of the nominative and adds ‑ν; the plural is **‑ᾱς** throughout, whatever the vowel of the singular.

### 2nd Declension (‑ο stems)

| Gender | Nominative | Accusative sg. | Accusative pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masculine | ὁ φίλος | τὸν φίλον | τοὺς φίλους |
| Feminine | ἡ ὁδός | τὴν ὁδόν | τὰς ὁδούς |
| Neuter | τὸ δῶρον | τὸ δῶρον | τὰ δῶρα |

Feminine ‑ος nouns (ἡ ὁδός, ἡ νῆσος) decline exactly like the masculines; only the article shows their gender. Neuters are identical to the nominative — as they are in every declension and every number.

### 3rd Declension (consonant and vowel stems)

The third declension builds its cases on the stem, and **the stem is found from the genitive singular, not from the nominative**: φύλαξ, φύλακ‑ος → stem φυλακ‑; σῶμα, σώματ‑ος → stem σωματ‑. Take the genitive, remove ‑ος, and add the accusative ending.

| Stem type | Nominative | Accusative sg. | Accusative pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Consonant stem | ὁ φύλαξ (φύλακος) | τὸν φύλακα | τοὺς φύλακας |
| ‑ρ stem | ὁ πατήρ (πατρός) | τὸν πατέρα | τοὺς πατέρας |
| Irregular ‑ρ stem | ὁ ἀνήρ (ἀνδρός) | τὸν ἄνδρα | τοὺς ἄνδρας |
| ι‑stem | ἡ πόλις (πόλεως) | τὴν πόλιν | τὰς πόλεις |
| ευ‑stem | ὁ βασιλεύς (βασιλέως) | τὸν βασιλέα | τοὺς βασιλέας |
| σ‑stem neuter | τὸ γένος (γένους) | τὸ γένος | τὰ γένη |
| Neuter consonant stem | τὸ σῶμα (σώματος) | τὸ σῶμα | τὰ σώματα |

_Key points_

- The ordinary masculine/feminine ending is **‑α** in the singular and **‑ας** in the plural.
- Stems ending in a **vowel** take ‑ν instead: πόλιν, ἰχθύν. Their plural is contracted: **πόλεις**, not *πόλιας.
- ‑ευ stems have **‑έα** in the singular (βασιλέα).
- Neuters, once more, repeat the nominative.

---

## 10. Points to Watch

- **Neuter forms are ambiguous between nominative and accusative.** Word order and the verb decide which: τὰ πράγματα may be subject or object.
- An accusative with no transitive verb in sight is usually one of the special uses above — respect, extent, oath, or the subject of an infinitive.

---

## 11. Historical Development

The accusative is the most stable case in Greek. Its endings barely move between Homer and the New Testament, and unlike the dative it never came under threat. What changes is how much work it is asked to do, and the trend is that it takes on **more**.

**Epic uses.** Two constructions are far commoner in Homer than in later prose:

- The **accusative of respect** (§6), naming the part or aspect in which something is true: πόδας ὠκὺς Ἀχιλλεύς "Achilles, swift **as to his feet**". Attic keeps it but uses it more sparingly.
- The **double accusative** (§2) with verbs of asking, teaching and depriving, which narrows steadily after the Classical period.

The accusative of extent in space and time (§5) runs through every period unchanged.

**Koine expands it — largely at the dative's expense.** As the dative recedes, prepositional phrases take over its jobs, and the preposition that gains most is **εἰς** + accusative:

| | Classical Attic | Koine tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Motion towards | εἰς + acc. | εἰς + acc. |
| Place where | ἐν + dative | ἐν + dat., but **εἰς + acc. encroaches** |
| Goal / recipient | dative | dative, or εἰς / πρός + acc. |

In the New Testament εἰς and ἐν are no longer kept rigidly apart, and εἰς sometimes appears where strict Attic would require ἐν with the dative. Greek is on its way to the modern state of affairs, in which the accusative is the ordinary case after almost every preposition.

**In Koine** the accusative is also the case that survives the general levelling best. Where third-declension nouns start drifting toward first-declension endings, it is usually the accusative plural that shows the first signs — the beginning of a merger that finishes long after the New Testament.

## 12. Example Sentences

| Greek | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **λέγε τὸν νόμον.** | Read out the law. *(direct object)* |
| **στρατηγὸν αὐτὸν εἵλοντο.** | They chose him general. *(predicate accusative)* |
| **νομίζω τοῦτο δίκαιον εἶναι.** | I consider this to be just. *(subject of the infinitive)* |
| **κατὰ τοῦτον τὸν νόμον ἥκω.** | I come in accordance with this law. *(after a preposition)* |
| **πολὺν χρόνον ἔμεινεν.** | He waited a long time. *(extent of time)* |
| **τί ταῦτα λέγεις;** | Why do you say this? *(adverbial neuter, and object)* |
| **μὰ Δία, οὐκ ἔγωγε.** | By Zeus, not I. *(oath)* |

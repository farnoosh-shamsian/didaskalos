# The Greek in This Book: Dialects and Periods

Every exercise sentence in this book is real. Nothing has been invented or simplified — each one was taken from a text that someone actually wrote. That is the strength of this method, and it has one consequence you should know about before you start: **the texts were not all written at the same time, or in the same kind of Greek.**

The authors in this collection are separated by as much as nine hundred years — Homer and the New Testament stand about that far apart. In that time the language changed — endings were lost, contractions happened, whole grammatical categories died out — and the writers also came from different regions, each with its own dialect. So you will meet forms that do not match the paradigms you have just been learning.

This module tells you where those forms come from, so that they read as *information* rather than as mistakes.

**How to read this module.** Skim it now — you have not yet read enough Greek for any of it to feel like a real problem, and it is not meant to be memorized on a first pass. Its value arrives later, on the day a line of Homer hands you πολέμοιο ⟨polémoio⟩ and the paradigm you learned says πολέμου ⟨polémou⟩. Come back to it every time a form surprises you, and especially to §4 and §6, which are the two sections you will use most often. Think of it as the reference page at the front of the book rather than a lesson to get through.

## 1. The Periods and Their Authors

| Period | Roughly | Kind of Greek | Authors in this collection |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Epic** | 750–650 BCE | Homeric — an artificial poetic language, mostly Ionic with older Aeolic forms mixed in | Homer, Homeric Hymns, Hesiod |
| **Ionic prose** | 5th c. BCE | Ionic | Herodotus |
| **Classical Attic** | 5th–4th c. BCE | Attic — the dialect of Athens | Thucydides, Plato, Xenophon, Aristotle, the orators (Lysias, Demosthenes, Aeschines, Antiphon), the tragedians (Aeschylus, Sophocles) |
| **Hellenistic** | 3rd–1st c. BCE | early Koine — a common Greek built on Attic, with a real Ionic ingredient | Polybius, Diodorus Siculus |
| **Imperial and New Testament** | 1st c. BCE – 3rd c. CE | Koine, and self-consciously archaic Attic | New Testament, Josephus, Plutarch, Lucian, Appian, Athenaeus, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Apollodorus, Aesop |

Treat the first row's dates loosely. The **Homeric Hymns** are a collection assembled over a long period — some are close to Homer in date, others are centuries later — and they are grouped here because they share his language and metre, not because they share his century. The same caution applies in the other direction to row five, as §3 explains.

The table is a sequence of *literary* periods, and it is worth knowing that the living dialects did not obligingly die off to make room for the next row. Long after Attic had become the standard of literary prose, the Greek states went on using their own dialects for their own documents — decrees, treaties, arbitrations — and went on doing so for one to three centuries after the Koine arrived. Nor is "Koine" a single thing: alongside the Attic Koine of this table there were a Doric Koine and a Northwest Greek Koine, each a regional standard in its own right. None of that is in your reading list, but it is why the picture of Greek narrowing steadily to one language is a simplification.

Two notes on the first row. Homer's language is usually described, accurately, as *Old Ionic with a retained layer of Aeolic forms* — ἄμμες ⟨ámmes⟩ beside ἡμεῖς ⟨hēmeîs⟩, a genitive singular in -ᾱο ⟨-āo⟩ beside -εω ⟨-eō⟩. Epic poetry seems in fact to have begun among the Aeolians of Asia Minor before passing to the neighbouring Ionians, which is why both layers are there. **Hesiod** is substantially the same language, but with some Aeolic forms Homer does not use and a few Boeotian and Doric ones besides — so he is not simply "Homer's dialect" the way the Hymns are.

## 2. The Reference Point, and How to Use the Dialect Notes

The opening module said that this book teaches **Attic** as its reference point. The reasons are worth having in front of you here: Attic is the dialect of most surviving classical prose, it is what nearly every grammar and dictionary is organized around, and Koine grew directly out of it. Every paradigm in every lesson is the Attic one unless that lesson says otherwise.

What does need saying is that the **Historical Development** section at the end of each grammar lesson is worth a different amount to different readers. Read it according to the corpus you chose:

| If your texts are… | Treat the dialect notes as… |
|---|---|
| Classical Attic prose and drama — Thucydides, Plato, Xenophon, the orators, the tragedians | **Background.** The paradigm in the lesson is already the Greek in front of you. Read the notes for context and etymology, and to know what you are *not* seeing; nothing in them is required to construe your sentences. |
| Hellenistic and Imperial Koine — Polybius, Plutarch, Josephus, Lucian | **Mostly background, with a few live differences.** Koine is built on Attic, so the paradigms hold, but the notes on σσ/ττ ⟨ss/tt⟩, the receding dative, the vanishing optative and the merging perfect describe your texts directly. |
| The New Testament | **Half background, half working knowledge.** The endings are largely Attic, but the syntactic notes — ἐν ⟨en⟩ doing the dative's work, ἵνα ⟨hína⟩ doing the infinitive's, the shrunken particle inventory — are about the language you are actually reading. |
| Homer, the Homeric Hymns, Hesiod, Herodotus | **Part of the lesson.** These notes are not extra; they are where your forms are. A Homeric line will hand you πολέμοιο, θεοῖσι ⟨polémoio, theoîsi⟩ and an uncontracted γένεος ⟨géneos⟩ that the paradigm table does not contain, and Herodotus will give you η ⟨ē⟩ where the table gives ᾱ ⟨ā⟩. Learn these sections as you learn the paradigm itself. |

The rule of thumb: **the further your authors are from fifth-century Athens, the more the dialect notes stop being commentary and start being the grammar you need.** If you chose a mixed corpus, expect both at once — and see §6 on using the citation to tell which is which.

## 3. Dialect Follows the Genre, Not the Author

One warning, because it will otherwise confuse you twice over.

**Not the date.** Several authors in the late group write deliberately archaic Attic, centuries after anyone spoke it. Lucian, Plutarch, Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Athenaeus all belong to a movement — **Atticism** — that treated the Attic of Plato and Demosthenes as the only proper literary Greek and imitated it closely. So a second-century CE author may write more conservatively than a first-century BCE one.

**Not the birthplace either.** Once each literary dialect had become the expected medium for a particular kind of writing, authors adopted it whatever they spoke at home. Choral lyric is in Doric whether the poet is a Dorian, a Boeotian like Pindar, or an Ionian like Simonides and Bacchylides. Hippocrates of Cos was a Dorian and wrote in Ionic, because Ionic was by then the language of prose enquiry.

So the governing variable is the **genre**, and neither the century nor the map will tell you the dialect. What will is the handful of signals below: learn them and let the forms speak for themselves.

## 4. The Six Signals You Will Actually Notice

These six account for the large majority of "why does this look wrong" moments. The first is far and away the commonest.

| Signal | Attic | Elsewhere | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **η for ᾱ** ⟨ē for ā⟩ | χώρᾱ, οἰκίᾱ, γενεᾱ́ ⟨khṓrā, oikíā, geneá⟩ | χώρη, οἰκίη, γενεή ⟨khṓrē, oikíē, geneḗ⟩ | Herodotus above all, and Homer — Ionic has η **even after ε, ι, ρ**, exactly where Attic has ᾱ |
| **σσ / ττ** ⟨ss / tt⟩ | θάλαττα, πράττω ⟨thálatta, práttō⟩ | θάλασσα, πράσσω ⟨thálassa, prássō⟩ | Homer, Herodotus, Koine, the NT — and Thucydides and the tragedians, who write σσ although their Greek is otherwise Attic (see the note below) |
| **ρρ / ρσ** ⟨rr / rs⟩ | θάρρος, ἄρρην ⟨thárros, árrēn⟩ | θάρσος, ἄρσην ⟨thársos, ársēn⟩ | Homer, Herodotus, Koine; also Thucydides and the tragedians |
| **Uncontracted vowels** | γένους, γένει ⟨génous, génei⟩ | γένεος, γένεϊ ⟨géneos, géneï⟩ | Homer and Herodotus — but in fact every dialect except Attic keeps these apart |
| **Longer datives** | θεοῖς, τιμαῖς, ποσί ⟨theoîs, timaîs, posí⟩ | θεοῖσι(ν), τιμῇσι, πόδεσσι ⟨theoîsi(n), timêisi, pódessi⟩ | Homer above all; Herodotus for -οισι ⟨-oisi⟩ and -ῃσι ⟨-ēisi⟩ |
| **Older genitive singulars** | πολέμου, Ἀτρεΐδου ⟨polémou, Atreḯdou⟩ | πολέμοιο, Ἀτρεΐδαο, δεσπότεω ⟨polémoio, Atreḯdao, despóteō⟩ | -οιο ⟨-oio⟩ and -ᾱο ⟨-āo⟩ in Homer; -εω ⟨-eō⟩ in Herodotus |

**Why Thucydides and the tragedians write σσ.** Not because Attic wavered: the inscriptions show Attic writing ττ and ρρ from the earliest times. σσ and ρσ are what the rest of Greece had, and the Athenian writers of the fifth century borrowed them from Ionic precisely *because* ττ and ρρ were felt to be a local Athenian peculiarity, unsuited to serious literature. So θάλασσα in a tragedy is a deliberate literary form, not an Attic variant — and the same explains why Koine, built on an Attic already shaped by Ionic, has σσ throughout.

A rough rule of thumb, with one caveat: **if a form looks longer or less contracted than the paradigm, it is probably the older one** — Homeric or Ionic. The caveat is that this is a fact about Attic and Ionic, not about Greek. The short datives -αις ⟨-ais⟩ and -οις ⟨-ois⟩ were original in most dialects; Attic itself used the long -ᾱσι ⟨-āsi⟩ and -οισι ⟨-oisi⟩ down to about 420 BCE. Use the rule for reading Homer and Herodotus against your Attic paradigm, which is what it is for, and do not generalise it into a law of the language.

## 5. Three Things That Change Across the Whole System

Beyond individual endings, three large shifts run through the entire language. Each is picked up again in the specific lessons where it matters; this is the overview.

**The dative recedes.** In Homer and Attic the bare dative does a great deal of work on its own — instrument, place, accompaniment, all with no preposition. Koine increasingly props it up with a preposition instead (ἐν ⟨en⟩ + dative, εἰς ⟨eis⟩ + accusative), and by Modern Greek the case is gone altogether. When a New Testament sentence uses ἐν ⟨en⟩ where Plato would have used a bare dative, this is why.

**The optative dies out.** The optative mood is common and supple in Homer and in Classical Attic *literature*. In the Hellenistic period it declines sharply, and in the New Testament it is essentially confined to a few fixed expressions — most famously μὴ γένοιτο ⟨mḕ génoito⟩ "may it not be so." If your corpus is mostly Koine you will see very few optatives; if it is Homer and Plato, you will see a great many.

The word *literature* is doing real work there. In the inscriptions — the everyday written language — the optative was already giving way to the subjunctive in conditional clauses during the classical period, in Attic-Ionic and in most other dialects alike. So the supple literary optative of Plato and Thucydides is the conservative exception, not the ordinary state of fifth-century Greek, and its later "collapse" is the end of a retreat that had begun long before.

**The perfect merges with the aorist.** Classical Greek keeps a sharp distinction: the aorist reports an event, the perfect reports a resulting state that still holds. In Koine that distinction erodes and the perfect is increasingly used as a plain past tense. The pluperfect, meanwhile, is progressively replaced by a periphrasis — participle plus a form of εἰμί ⟨eimí⟩.

## 6. Reading the Citations

Every exercise sentence in this book is followed by a short reference in italics — *Hom. Il. 1.1-1.7*, *Hdt. 1.1*, *Thuc. 2.40*, *John 3*, *Lys. 12.1*. These are the standard scholarly abbreviations, and they are the tool that makes everything above usable.

When a form surprises you, look at the citation first:

- *Hom. Il.*, *Hom. Od.*, *Hes. Theog.*, *Hymn. Hom. Cer.* — epic. Expect uncontracted vowels, long datives in -οισι/-ῃσι/-εσσι ⟨-oisi/-ēisi/-essi⟩, genitives in -οιο and -ᾱο ⟨-oio, -āo⟩, and an article that is still really a demonstrative.
- *Hdt.* — Herodotus, in Ionic. Expect η ⟨ē⟩ for Attic ᾱ ⟨ā⟩, σσ ⟨ss⟩ for ττ ⟨tt⟩, uncontracted forms, and genitives in -εω and -έων ⟨-eō, -éōn⟩. A handful of individual words differ too, and they are worth knowing on sight because they are common: **κεῖνος** ⟨keînos⟩ for ἐκεῖνος "that", **ἤν** ⟨ḗn⟩ for ἐάν "if", **μέζων** ⟨mézōn⟩ for μείζων "greater", **ἱρός** ⟨hirós⟩ beside ἱερός "holy", **ἑωυτοῦ** ⟨heōutoû⟩ for ἑαυτοῦ "of himself", and **μιν** ⟨min⟩ where Attic would say αὐτόν.
- *Thuc.*, *Pl. Ap.*, *Xen. Hell.*, *Arist. Pol.*, *Dem.*, *Lys.*, *Aeschin.*, *Antiph.* — Classical Attic prose. This is the paradigm as the lesson gives it, except that Thucydides writes σσ and ρσ for Attic ττ and ρρ, and ἐς for εἰς.
- *Soph. Ant.*, *Aesch. Ag.* and the other tragedies — Attic verse. The tragedians write σσ and ρσ for Attic ττ and ρρ, and their choral odes use a Doric-flavored literary language: expect long ᾱ where Attic prose has η. That "Doric" is not the dialect of any actual Dorian city; it is an artificial composite, carrying the general Doric features and shedding the local ones.
- *Polyb.*, *Diod. Sic.* — Hellenistic Koine, still close to Attic.
- *Matt.*, *Mark*, *Luke*, *John*, *Acts*, *Rom.*, *Heb.*, *Rev.* and the other New Testament books — Koine. Expect σσ ⟨ss⟩, few optatives, prepositions doing work the dative once did, and a very productive -μα/-ματος ⟨-ma/-matos⟩ noun class.
- *Plut.*, *Luc. VH*, *Joseph. BJ*, *App. BC*, *Ath.*, *Dion. Hal.*, *Apollod.*, *Aesop* — Imperial period. Ranges from plain Koine (Aesop, Apollodorus) to careful Atticism (Lucian, Plutarch, Dionysius) — see §3.

You do not need to memorize this table. You need only the habit of glancing at the citation before deciding that a form is irregular. Nine times out of ten it is perfectly regular — for the dialect it belongs to.

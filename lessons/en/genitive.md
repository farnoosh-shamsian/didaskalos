# The Genitive Case

The **genitive** is the case of relation. Its commonest job is to attach one noun to another — ὁ τοῦ ἀνδρὸς λόγος "the man's argument" — but it does much more than possession: it names the whole from which a part is taken, the thing one is separated from, the agent of a passive verb, and the standard something is measured against. It is also the case that a large family of Greek verbs governs instead of an accusative object.

Two older cases lie behind it, and that is why its range looks so wide. Greek merged the inherited genitive ("of") with the **ablative** ("from"), so one set of endings covers both belonging and separation. When a genitive resists translation with "of", try "from".

---

## 1. Possession and Connection

The plain genitive links noun to noun. It normally sits **inside the article phrase** of the noun it belongs to, or immediately after it.

- **ὁ τοῦ ἀνδρὸς λόγος** — "the man's argument."
- **ἡ τῶν Ἀθηναίων πόλις** — "the city of the Athenians."
- **ὁ πατὴρ αὐτοῦ** — "his father."

The relation need not be ownership. It may be authorship (τὰ Ὁμήρου "Homer's works"), material, quality, or simply association: τὰ τῆς πόλεως "the affairs of the city".

**Subjective and objective.** ὁ τῶν πολεμίων φόβος can mean either "the enemy's fear" (they are afraid) or "fear of the enemy" (we fear them). Greek does not distinguish the two; only the sense of the passage does.

---

## 2. Partitive Genitive

The genitive names the **whole** out of which a part is taken.

- **πολλοὶ τῶν πολιτῶν** — "many of the citizens."
- **οὐδεὶς ἡμῶν** — "none of us."
- **τῆς νυκτός** — "during the night" (i.e. within some part of it).

Words of quantity, superlatives, and the neuter of adjectives all attract it: τὸ πλῆθος τῶν στρατιωτῶν, ἄριστος τῶν Ἑλλήνων "best of the Greeks", εἰς τοῦτο ἀνοίας "to this (pitch) of folly".

---

## 3. Separation, Comparison, Value

These are the old ablative jobs.

- **Separation** with verbs of ceasing, depriving, keeping away: **παύομαι τοῦ πολέμου** "I cease from the war."
- **Comparison** after a comparative, where English uses "than": **μείζων τούτου** "greater than this." The alternative is ἤ with the same case as the first term.
- **Value and price**: **πολλοῦ ἄξιος** "worth much", **δέκα μνῶν ἐπρίατο** "he bought it for ten minae."
- **Charge** in a lawsuit: **φόνου διώκειν** "to prosecute on a charge of murder."

---

## 4. After Prepositions

The genitive follows every preposition that expresses **"from"** or **"about"**, and it is one of the cases taken by most of the two- and three-case prepositions.

- **ἐκ τῆς πόλεως** "out of the city" · **ἀπὸ τῆς θαλάττης** "from the sea" · **πρὸ τῆς μάχης** "before the battle"
- **περὶ τοῦ νόμου** "about the law" · **ὑπὲρ τῆς πατρίδος** "on behalf of the fatherland" · **μετὰ τῶν φίλων** "with his friends"

**ὑπό + genitive** deserves separate notice: it marks the **agent of a passive verb** — φιλεῖται ὑπὸ τῶν θεῶν "he is loved **by** the gods." You will meet it constantly.

---

## 5. Verbs Governing the Genitive

A verb's object is not always accusative. Learn the case with the verb; these families take a genitive:

| Meaning | Verbs |
| --- | --- |
| hearing, perceiving (the source) | ἀκούω, αἰσθάνομαι, πυνθάνομαι |
| remembering, forgetting | μέμνημαι, ἐπιλανθάνομαι |
| desiring, aiming at | ἐπιθυμέω, τυγχάνω, ἅπτομαι |
| ruling, leading | ἄρχω, ἡγέομαι, κρατέω |
| beginning, ceasing | ἄρχομαι, παύομαι |
| accusing, condemning | κατηγορέω, καταψηφίζομαι |

Note the pair ἀκούω τοῦ ἀνδρός "I hear the man (speaking)" beside ἀκούω τὸν λόγον "I hear the words" — genitive for the source of the sound, accusative for the thing heard.

---

## 6. Time and Place

The bare genitive marks the **time within which** something happens, as against the dative (a point in time) and the accusative (a stretch of time).

- **νυκτός** "by night, in the course of the night" · **τριῶν ἡμερῶν** "within three days"
- Compare: **ταύτῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ** "on that day" (dative) · **τρεῖς ἡμέρας** "for three days" (accusative)

---

## 7. The Genitive Absolute

A noun or pronoun in the genitive with a participle agreeing with it forms a self-contained clause, grammatically detached from the rest of the sentence. English uses a subordinate clause.

- **τούτων λεχθέντων, ἀπῆλθον.** — "When this had been said, they departed."
- **τοῦ στρατηγοῦ κελεύοντος, ἐμάχοντο.** — "Since the general ordered it, they fought."

The construction is used when the participle's subject is **not** a word in the main clause; if it is, the participle simply agrees with that word in its own case. Time, cause, condition and concession are all expressed this way, and context alone decides which.

## 8. The Endings, Declension by Declension

### 1st Declension (‑ᾱ/‑ᾰ/‑η stems)

| Type | Nominative | Genitive sg. | Genitive pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feminine in ‑η | ἡ τιμή | τῆς τιμῆς | τῶν τιμῶν |
| Feminine in ‑ᾰ/‑ᾱ (after ε, ι, ρ) | ἡ χώρα | τῆς χώρας | τῶν χωρῶν |
| Feminine in ‑ᾰ (other) | ἡ θάλαττα | τῆς θαλάττης | τῶν θαλαττῶν |
| **Masculine in ‑ης** | ὁ πολίτης | **τοῦ πολίτου** | τῶν πολιτῶν |
| **Masculine in ‑ᾱς** | ὁ νεανίας | **τοῦ νεανίου** | τῶν νεανιῶν |

_Key points_

- The feminine genitive singular is **‑ᾱς** when the nominative ends in ‑ᾱ after ε, ι or ρ (χώρας, οἰκίας); otherwise **‑ης** (τιμῆς, θαλάττης, δίκης).
- **The masculines borrow ‑ου from the second declension**: πολίτης → πολίτου, δικαστής → δικαστοῦ, στρατιώτης → στρατιώτου. This is the one place where a first-declension noun does not look like a first-declension noun, and it catches beginners out.
- The genitive plural is **‑ῶν** with a circumflex throughout the declension, whatever the accent of the other forms.

### 2nd Declension (‑ο stems)

| Gender | Nominative | Genitive sg. | Genitive pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masculine | ὁ φίλος | τοῦ φίλου | τῶν φίλων |
| Feminine | ἡ ὁδός | τῆς ὁδοῦ | τῶν ὁδῶν |
| Neuter | τὸ δῶρον | τοῦ δώρου | τῶν δώρων |

The accent stays where the nominative had it, as far as the accent rules allow.

### 3rd Declension (consonant and vowel stems)

The genitive singular is the key form of this declension: it is where the true stem shows itself, which is why third-declension nouns are always learned as **nominative + genitive** (σῶμα, σώματος; ἀνήρ, ἀνδρός). Every other case is built on the stem the genitive reveals.

| Stem type | Nominative | Genitive sg. | Genitive pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Consonant stem | ὁ φύλαξ | τοῦ φύλακος | τῶν φυλάκων |
| ‑ρ stem | ὁ πατήρ | τοῦ πατρός | τῶν πατέρων |
| Irregular ‑ρ stem | ὁ ἀνήρ | τοῦ ἀνδρός | τῶν ἀνδρῶν |
| Neuter consonant stem | τὸ σῶμα | τοῦ σώματος | τῶν σωμάτων |
| ι‑stem | ἡ πόλις | τῆς πόλεως | τῶν πόλεων |
| ευ‑stem | ὁ βασιλεύς | τοῦ βασιλέως | τῶν βασιλέων |
| σ‑stem neuter | τὸ γένος | τοῦ γένους | τῶν γενῶν |

_Key points_

- The ordinary ending is **‑ος** in the singular and **‑ων** in the plural.
- ι‑stems and ευ‑stems have **‑εως / ‑εων**, and the ι‑stems keep their accent on the antepenult (πόλεως, πόλεων) — a licensed exception to the ordinary accent rule.
- σ‑stem neuters contract: γένε(σ)ος → **γένους**.

## 9. Summary of Endings

| Declension | Genitive sg. | Genitive pl. |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st feminine | ‑ης / ‑ᾱς | ‑ῶν |
| 1st masculine | ‑ου | ‑ῶν |
| 2nd | ‑ου | ‑ων |
| 3rd | ‑ος (ι‑/ευ‑stems ‑εως, σ‑stems ‑ους) | ‑ων (ι‑stems ‑εων) |

---

## 10. Points to Watch

- **τῶν is the genitive plural of all three genders** — the article tells you nothing about gender here.
- ‑ου is both a second-declension genitive singular and a first-declension **masculine** genitive singular; both are genitive, so no harm done.
- A genitive standing between article and noun is attributive (ἡ τῶν Ἀθηναίων πόλις); a genitive of αὐτός stands **outside** it (ἡ γυνὴ αὐτοῦ "his wife").
- When a genitive has no noun to attach to and no preposition, look for one of §3, §5, §6 or §7.

---

## 11. Historical Development

Like the dative, the Greek genitive is a merger of two older cases: the inherited genitive ("of") and the **ablative** ("from"). That is why one ending covers both possession (τοῦ φίλου "of the friend") and separation (ἀπὸ τῆς πόλεως "from the city"), and why verbs of depriving, ceasing and differing all take it.

**The singular endings you learned are the shortened ones.** The older, longer forms are everywhere in epic and Ionic:

| | Homer | Ionic (Herodotus) | Attic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st decl. masc. sg. | ‑ᾱο (Ἀτρεΐδαο) | ‑εω (δεσπότεω) | ‑ου |
| 1st decl. plural | ‑άων (θεάων) | ‑έων | ‑ῶν |
| 2nd decl. sg. | **‑οιο** (πολέμοιο), also ‑ου | ‑ου | ‑ου |
| σ-stem sg. | γένεος | γένεος | γένους |
| ι-stem sg. | πόληος, πόλιος | πόλιος | πόλεως |

**‑οιο** is worth singling out: it is one of the first endings that marks a text as epic.

**Usage shifts.** The **partitive** genitive (§2) is markedly more frequent in Homer than in Attic prose, and often stands alone where later Greek would add a preposition. Moving the other way, Koine increasingly prefers to make the relation explicit with a preposition: **ἐκ** or **ἀπό** + genitive where Attic was content with the bare case. The **genitive of comparison** (§3) likewise gives ground in Koine to **ἤ** "than" or **παρά** / **ὑπέρ** + accusative — a construction familiar to readers of the New Testament.

**The genitive absolute** (§7), on the other hand, thrives: already common in Attic, it is used freely throughout Koine narrative.

**In Koine** the genitive is the one oblique case that gains ground. As the dative recedes, the genitive absorbs some of its territory.

## 12. Example Sentences

| Greek | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **ὁ τῶν Ἀθηναίων δῆμος** | the people of the Athenians *(possession)* |
| **πολλοὶ τῶν πολιτῶν** | many of the citizens *(partitive)* |
| **μείζων τούτου** | greater than this *(comparison)* |
| **περὶ τοῦ νόμου λέγω.** | I am speaking about the law. *(after a preposition)* |
| **ταῦτα ὑπὸ πάντων λέγεται.** | This is said by everyone. *(agent)* |
| **ἀκούω τοῦ ἀνδρός.** | I hear the man. *(verb governing the genitive)* |
| **τούτων λεχθέντων, ἀπῆλθον.** | When this had been said, they left. *(genitive absolute)* |

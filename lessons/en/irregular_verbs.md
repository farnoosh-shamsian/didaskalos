# Irregular Verbs

Most Greek verbs announce their conjugation in their dictionary form: **‑ω** (λύω) and **‑ομαι** (βούλομαι) follow the thematic paradigms, **‑μι** and **‑μαι** (δίδωμι, δύναμαι) the athematic ones. A small group fits neither, and it is a group you cannot avoid: these words are among the most frequent verbs in Greek.

There is no "irregular conjugation" to learn. What follows is three families of verbs, each defective in its own way, and each best learned as vocabulary rather than as a paradigm.

| Family | Members | Why they stand apart |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Suppletive aorists | εἶπον, εἶδον, ἦλθον, ἤνεγκον | The aorist is built on a different root from the present |
| Perfects with present meaning | οἶδα, ἔοικα, ἄνωγα, μέμαα | Perfect in form, present in force |
| Impersonals | δεῖ, χρή, ἔξεστι | Third person singular only; no personal subject |

---

## 1. Suppletive Aorists

Some verbs supply their aorist from an entirely different root. This is called **suppletion**: two unrelated stems are pressed into service as though they were one verb, so that ὁράω "I see" takes the aorist εἶδον. The present and the aorist are two roots serving one meaning, and both must be learned as a pair.

| Present | Aorist | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| λέγω "say" | **εἶπον** | "I said" |
| ὁράω "see" | **εἶδον** | "I saw" |
| ἔρχομαι "come, go" | **ἦλθον** | "I came, went" |
| φέρω "carry" | **ἤνεγκον / ἤνεγκα** | "I carried" |
| ἐσθίω "eat" | **ἔφαγον** | "I ate" |

These aorists take the **thematic aorist (thematic) endings** — the same endings as the imperfect. What marks them as aorist is the *stem*, not the ending:

| Person | εἶπον "I said" | εἶδον "I saw" |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 sg | εἶπον | εἶδον |
| 2 sg | εἶπες | εἶδες |
| 3 sg | εἶπε(ν) | εἶδε(ν) |
| 1 pl | εἴπομεν | εἴδομεν |
| 2 pl | εἴπετε | εἴδετε |
| 3 pl | εἶπον | εἶδον |

Infinitives **εἰπεῖν**, **ἰδεῖν**; participles **εἰπών, εἰποῦσα, εἰπόν** and **ἰδών, ἰδοῦσα, ἰδόν**.

> **Watch the compounds.** εἶπον and εἶδον are extremely productive: προσεῖπον "addressed", προεῖπον "proclaimed", ἀπεῖπον "refused", ἀντεῖπον "spoke against", κατεῖδον "caught sight of". If a form looks like εἶπον or εἶδον with a preposition in front, it is one of these.

---

## 2. Perfects with Present Meaning

A few verbs are perfect in form but present in meaning: to *have seen* is to know, to *have come to resemble* is to resemble.

**οἶδα** "I know" (root ἰδ‑/οἰδ‑/εἰδ‑, the same root as εἶδον) is the one to memorize completely. Note the stem change between singular (οἰδ‑) and plural (ἰσ‑):

| Person | Form | Gloss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 sg | **οἶδα** | I know |
| 2 sg | **οἶσθα** | you know |
| 3 sg | **οἶδε(ν)** | he/she/it knows |
| 1 pl | **ἴσμεν** | we know |
| 2 pl | **ἴστε** | you (pl.) know |
| 3 pl | **ἴσασι(ν)** | they know |

Infinitive **εἰδέναι**, participle **εἰδώς, εἰδυῖα, εἰδός**, past (pluperfect in form) **ᾔδη / ᾔδειν** "I knew", future **εἴσομαι** "I shall know".

The others work the same way: **ἔοικα** "I seem, I resemble" (+ dative), **σύνοιδα** "I share knowledge, I am conscious" (+ dative), and in epic and tragedy **ἄνωγα** "I command" (3 sg ἀνώγει) and **μέμαα** "I am eager".

## 3. Impersonal Verbs

Impersonals have **no personal subject** — the subject is the situation itself. They appear only in the third person singular. The person who must or may act stands in the **accusative** (with δεῖ, χρή) or **dative** (with ἔξεστι), followed by an infinitive.

| Verb | Construction | Example | Translation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **δεῖ** | acc. + inf. | δεῖ ἡμᾶς μάχεσθαι | "we must fight" |
| **χρή** | acc. + inf. | χρή σε λέγειν | "you must speak" |
| **ἔξεστι** | dat. + inf. | ἔξεστί σοι μένειν | "you may stay" |

Their small sets of forms:

| | Present | Imperfect | Future | Infinitive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| δεῖ | δεῖ | ἔδει | δεήσει | δεῖν |
| χρή | χρή | χρῆν / ἐχρῆν | χρῆσται (rare) | χρῆναι |
| ἔξεστι | ἔξεστι(ν) | ἐξῆν | ἐξέσται | ἐξεῖναι |

> **Nuance:** δεῖ and χρή both mean "it is necessary", but χρή leans toward *moral obligation* ("one ought"), δεῖ toward *practical necessity* ("it is needful"). δεῖ + genitive means "there is need of": δεῖ χρημάτων "money is needed".

Also common: **εἰκός (ἐστι)** "it is likely", **δοκεῖ** "it seems (good)", **μέλει** "it is a concern" (+ dative).

## 4. Key Rules and Common Pitfalls

| Rule | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Learn the pair, not the paradigm** | For a suppletive verb, memorize present and aorist together: λέγω / εἶπον, ὁράω / εἶδον. Neither one predicts the other. |
| **2. Thematic aorist endings are imperfect endings** | εἶπον and ἐλάμβανον share their endings. The stem tells you the tense: ἔλαβον (aorist) vs. ἐλάμβανον (imperfect). |
| **3. οἶσθα, not οἶδας** | The 2nd singular of οἶδα is οἶσθα in Attic; οἶδας is later Greek. |
| **4. The plural of οἶδα changes stem** | Singular οἰδ‑, plural ἰσ‑: ἴσμεν, ἴστε, ἴσασι. Do not build these from λύω endings. |
| **5. ἴστε is ambiguous** | ἴστε = "you know" (indicative) or "know!" (imperative). Context decides. |
| **6. Impersonals stay in the 3rd singular** | Never look for δεῖς or χρῇς — the person appears as an accusative or dative, not in the verb ending. |
| **7. χρῆν is not χρῆσθαι** | χρῆν = χρή + ἦν "it was necessary"; χρῆσθαι "to use" belongs to χράομαι, a different verb. |

## 5. Example Sentences

- **εἶπε δὲ ταῦτα ὁ Κῦρος.** — "And Cyrus said this."
- **εἶδον τοὺς πολεμίους προσιόντας.** — "They saw the enemy approaching."
- **οἶδα οὐδὲν εἰδώς.** — "I know that I know nothing." (the Socratic paradox)
- **ἴσασιν οἱ θεοὶ τὰ μέλλοντα.** — "The gods know what is to come."
- **δεῖ σε ἀληθῆ λέγειν.** — "You must tell the truth."
- **χρὴ τοὺς πολίτας τοῖς νόμοις πείθεσθαι.** — "The citizens must obey the laws."
- **ἔξεστιν ὑμῖν εἰρήνην ἄγειν.** — "You are permitted to live in peace."

## 6. Historical Development

There is a reason the irregular verbs are also the commonest ones. **Frequency protects irregularity**: a verb used constantly is learned as a whole, form by form, and never gets levelled by analogy, while a rare verb is rebuilt on the regular pattern the moment a speaker has to guess. This is why the irregular verbs of any language tend to be its everyday ones — "be", "go", "see", "give" — and Greek is no exception: εἰμί, ἔρχομαι, ὁράω and δίδωμι are all both irregular and unavoidable.

**Suppletion is old.** The suppletive verbs are among the oldest material in the language: ἔρχομαι / ἦλθον / ἐλήλυθα and ὁράω / εἶδον / ὄψομαι are each built from two or three originally separate roots that were pressed into one paradigm long before Greek was written down. **οἶδα** "I know" is a genuine relic — an old perfect of the root meaning "see", so literally "I have seen, therefore I know", as Greek's own εἶδον still shows.

**Homer has even more variety.** Epic preserves alternative forms that Attic dropped: **ἔμμεναι, ἔμεν, ἔμμεν** beside εἶναι; **ἴδμεν** for ἴσμεν "we know"; **ἔσσομαι** for ἔσομαι. Since these are the highest-frequency verbs in the language, an unfamiliar epic form here will block a sentence more often than an unfamiliar noun would — they are worth meeting early if you are reading Homer.

**Koine starts to regularize — visibly.** The clearest case is the **α-aorist**, in which the endings of the first (sigmatic) aorist are transferred onto thematic aorist stems:

| Classical | Koine / NT |
|---|---|
| εἶπον, εἶπες (εἶπα rare) | **εἶπα, εἶπας** |
| ἦλθον, ἤλθετε | **ἦλθα, ἤλθατε** |
| εἶδον | **εἶδα** (occasionally) |

Both forms coexist in the New Testament, often within a few verses. This is not corruption; it is the regular pattern winning, one verb at a time.

## 7. Usage Notes

1. **Frequency.** εἶπον, εἶδον, οἶδα, δεῖ and χρή are among the most common verbs in all of Greek prose. The handful of forms in this lesson will earn their keep on every page.
2. **οἶδα vs. γιγνώσκω.** οἶδα is knowing a fact; γιγνώσκω is coming to know, recognizing. σύνοιδα + dative = "I am conscious of, I share the knowledge with".
3. **Indirect questions.** οἶδα is everywhere in them: οὐκ οἶδα ὅστις ἐστίν "I do not know who he is".
4. **Reading strategy.** When a verb form resists analysis, check this list before assuming a rare paradigm. It is far more often εἶπον under a preposition than an exotic ending.

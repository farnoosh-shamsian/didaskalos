# Conditional Sentences

---

## 1. Two Clauses, Not One Form

Every lesson before this one teaches a **form**: a set of endings that marks a tense, a mood, a case. A conditional is not a form. Neither half of a conditional sentence looks unusual on its own — the verbs are the indicatives, subjunctives and optatives you already know. What makes the sentence a conditional is the **relation between two clauses**, and what distinguishes one type from another is which mood stands in each clause.

The two halves have names worth learning, because every grammar uses them:

- the **protasis** is the "if" clause, introduced by **εἰ** or **ἐάν**;
- the **apodosis** is the clause that says what follows.

You now know the present and imperfect indicative, the aorist, the subjunctive and the optative. That is everything the system is built from, which is why this lesson appears here and not earlier.

---

## 2. ἄν

Before the types, one small word.

**ἄν** has no meaning of its own and no translation. It marks a verb as **conditioned** — as holding not absolutely but under some circumstance, stated or implied. It never stands first in its clause: it is postpositive, attaching to the preceding word, most often a negative, an interrogative or an emphatic word.

| With | Force | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **optative** | *potential*: "would", "might" | οὐκ ἂν λέγοιμι — "I would not say" |
| **subjunctive**, in a subordinate clause | *indefinite*: "whenever", "whoever" | ὅταν ἔλθῃ (= ὅτε + ἄν) — "whenever he comes" |
| **past indicative** | *contrary to fact*, or *past habitual* | ἔλεγεν ἄν — "he would be saying", or "he used to say" |

A verb that would be a plain statement becomes a possibility with ἄν and nothing else: λέγοι is "may he speak"; λέγοι ἄν is "he would speak". Its absence is equally informative — an optative without ἄν in a main clause is a wish, not a possibility.

**ἐάν is εἰ with ἄν fused onto it.** That is why ἐάν always takes the subjunctive: the ἄν inside it is doing the work of the second row above. Watch for its other shapes: Ionic and the older Attic writers use ἤν, and Plato and later writers often use a bare ἄ̄ν — with a long α, and not to be confused with the modal particle.

## 3. The Types

| Type | Protasis | Apodosis | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple (particular)** | εἰ + indicative | indicative | states a fact, taking no view on whether it holds |
| **Present general** | ἐάν + subjunctive | present indicative | "whenever this happens, that happens" |
| **Past general** | εἰ + optative | imperfect indicative | "whenever this happened, that happened" |
| **Future more vivid** | ἐάν + subjunctive | future indicative | a likely future case |
| **Future less vivid** | εἰ + optative | optative + ἄν | a remoter future case: "should … would" |
| **Present contrary to fact** | εἰ + imperfect indicative | imperfect + ἄν | "if it were so now — but it is not" |
| **Past contrary to fact** | εἰ + aorist indicative | aorist + ἄν | "if it had been so — but it was not" |

The difference between the aorist and the imperfect in the last two rows is aspectual; the present/past split shown here is a rule of thumb, and an imperfect can refer to continued past time.

Reading them, the useful question is not "which conjunction?" but **"is there an ἄν in the apodosis?"** Its presence marks the unreal and the remote; its absence marks the simple and the general.

- εἰ ταῦτα λέγεις, ἁμαρτάνεις. — "If you say this, you are wrong." (simple)
- ἐὰν ταῦτα λέγῃς, ἁμαρτήσῃ. — "If you say this, you will be wrong." (future more vivid)
- εἰ ταῦτα λέγοις, ἁμαρτάνοις ἄν. — "If you should say this, you would be wrong." (future less vivid)
- εἰ ταῦτα ἔλεγες, ἡμάρτανες ἄν. — "If you were saying this, you would be wrong." (present contrary to fact)

---

## 4. The Two Generals

The general conditions are the pair most easily missed, because neither carries an ἄν in the apodosis and both look like ordinary statements.

They describe a **repeated** case rather than a single one — "whenever", not "if". The protasis takes the subjunctive when the apodosis is present, and the optative when the apodosis is past. This is the same primary/secondary shift that governs purpose clauses and indirect statement, so it is worth recognizing here: a subordinate subjunctive moves to the optative when the main verb is past.

- ἐὰν τις ἔλθῃ, δεχόμεθα αὐτόν. — "Whenever anyone comes, we receive him." (present general)
- εἴ τις ἔλθοι, ἐδεχόμεθα αὐτόν. — "Whenever anyone came, we received him." (past general)

An optative in a protasis is therefore ambiguous on its own: with an optative and ἄν in the apodosis it is a future less vivid, with an imperfect indicative it is a past general. The apodosis decides.

---

## 5. Points to Watch

1. **The negative of the protasis is μή, not οὐ.** εἰ μὴ τοῦτο λέγεις, "if you do not say this". The apodosis keeps οὐ. A μή after εἰ is not a separate construction; it is simply how a conditional says "not".
2. **The order is free.** The apodosis often comes first, and the protasis is frequently embedded inside it: ἁμαρτάνοις ἄν, εἰ ταῦτα λέγοις.
3. **Mixed conditions are common.** Nothing requires both halves to come from the same row. A past contrary-to-fact protasis with a present contrary-to-fact apodosis says "if it had happened then, things would be otherwise now", and Greek says it freely.
4. **εἰ does not always mean "if".** After verbs of asking, knowing and wondering it means "whether" and introduces an indirect question: ἐρωτᾷ εἰ ἥκει, "he asks whether he has come". The tables below exclude these.
5. **εἴτε … εἴτε is "whether … or"**, a disjunction, not a condition.
6. **The pluperfect also appears** in the past contrary to fact, beside the aorist. It is uncommon, and the aorist is the form to expect.

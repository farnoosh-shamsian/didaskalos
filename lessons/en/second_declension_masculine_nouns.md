# Second Declension Masculine Nouns (o-stems)

Second declension nouns in **-ος** are the single most common noun type in Greek prose. Most are masculine: λόγος "word", φίλος "friend", ἄνθρωπος "human being". A few are feminine but decline identically — ἡ ὁδός "road", ἡ νῆσος "island" — and only the article reveals their gender.

## How to recognize the class

- Nominative singular in **-ος**, genitive singular in **-ου**.
- Dictionary entry: λόγος, -ου, ὁ.

## Paradigm

λόγος "word":

| Case | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | λόγος | λόγοι |
| Genitive | λόγου | λόγων |
| Dative | λόγῳ | λόγοις |
| Accusative | λόγον | λόγους |
| Vocative | λόγε | λόγοι |

## Common vocabulary

| Noun | Genitive | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ἄνθρωπος | ἀνθρώπου | human being |
| θεός | θεοῦ | god |
| νόμος | νόμου | law, custom |
| πόλεμος | πολέμου | war |
| δῆμος | δήμου | people |
| ἵππος | ἵππου | horse |
| ἡ ὁδός | ὁδοῦ | road, way (feminine!) |

## Points to watch

- **Learn each noun with its article.** ὁ λόγος but ἡ ὁδός — the ending alone does not tell you the gender.
- The vocative singular in -ε is distinctive: ὦ φίλε "friend!" (θεός usually keeps θεός in the vocative.)
- Contract nouns like νοῦς "mind" (from νόος) belong here too: νοῦς, νοῦ, νῷ, νοῦν.
- Do not confuse the accusative singular -ον with the neuter nouns of the next lesson; for masculines, nominative -ος and accusative -ον differ, while neuters use -ον for both.

## Historical Development

The second declension is the most stable pattern in the language — but two of its endings are among the first things you will notice in Homer.

| Case | Homer | Ionic (Herodotus) | Attic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genitive singular | **-οιο** (πολέμοιο, ἵπποιο), also -ου | -ου | -ου (πολέμου) |
| Dative plural | **-οισι(ν)** (θεοῖσι), also -οις | -οισι | -οις (θεοῖς) |

**-οιο is the single most recognizable epic ending.** It is the older genitive, and Homer uses it constantly beside the shorter -ου, choosing whichever the metre wants. Once you know it, a great deal of Homer stops looking strange: πολέμοιο is simply "of war."

**-οισι** works the same way. Homer has both θεοῖσι and θεοῖς; Herodotus prefers -οισι; Attic prose has settled on -οις. Two cautions against reading that as a simple march from long to short. Attic inscriptions wrote -οισι until about 444 BCE, so this is a difference between *later fifth-century* Attic and the rest, not between Attic and its own past. And in most Greek dialects -οις is the only form there ever was, so the long ending is a property of Attic-Ionic, Lesbian and epic rather than an inheritance the others lost.

**The Attic declension is a local development.** A small group of nouns shifts in Attic to -ως / -ων: **νεώς** "temple", **λεώς** "people", the name Μενέλεως. It is called *Attic* from the contrast with Koine, which has -ος throughout; the change behind it — two vowels exchanging length, the first shortening as the second lengthens — is Attic-Ionic, and Herodotus has these forms too. What the other dialects have depends on how far each took that change:

| | Form |
|---|---|
| Attic and Herodotus | νεώς, λεώς |
| Homer | νηός — the intermediate stage, with η not yet shortened |
| Most other dialects | ναός, λαός — the original, untouched |
| Koine | **ναός**, **λαός** |

Homer's own λαός belongs with the third row: it is one of the non-Ionic forms his language carries. Since λαός "people" is extremely frequent in the Septuagint and the New Testament, this is a difference you will meet early if your corpus includes them.

**In Koine** the declension is unchanged and remains the default pattern for masculine nouns: λόγος, θεός, κύριος, ἄνθρωπος, οὐρανός.

## Examples

- ὁ νόμος τοὺς ἀνθρώπους παιδεύει — "the law educates people"
- ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ — "on the road" (feminine article + dative)
- οἱ τοῦ δήμου λόγοι — "the words of the people"

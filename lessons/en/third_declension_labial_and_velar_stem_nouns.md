# Third Declension Labial and Velar Stem Nouns (-ψ / -ξ)

These nouns attach the shared third declension endings to a stem ending in a **stop**: a labial (π, β, φ) or a velar (κ, γ, χ). When the ending -ς meets that stop, the stop is not lost — it fuses with the σ and is still written, as **ψ** or **ξ**:

- φυλακ- + ς → **φύλαξ**, φύλακ-ος "guard" (stem φυλακ-)
- φλεβ- + ς → **φλέψ**, φλεβ-ός "vein" (stem φλεβ-)

The nominative singular therefore tells you *that* the stem ends in a stop, and the genitive singular tells you *which* stop it is. Learn the two forms together; the genitive is the one that cannot mislead you.

Gender is not predictable from the endings; learn it with the article.

## The shared endings

| Case | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | -ς | -ες |
| Genitive | -ος | -ων |
| Dative | -ι | -σι(ν) |
| Accusative | -α | -ας |
| Vocative | = nominative | -ες |

## Paradigms

φύλαξ "guard" (velar stem φυλακ-) and φλέψ "vein" (labial stem φλεβ-):

| Case | Singular | Plural | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | φύλαξ | φύλακες | φλέψ | φλέβες |
| Genitive | φύλακος | φυλάκων | φλεβός | φλεβῶν |
| Dative | φύλακι | φύλαξι(ν) | φλεβί | φλεψί(ν) |
| Accusative | φύλακα | φύλακας | φλέβα | φλέβας |
| Vocative | φύλαξ | φύλακες | φλέψ | φλέβες |

## Sound changes before -σ

The nominative singular -ς and the dative plural -σι(ν) are the only two places where the stop meets a σ:

| Stem ends in | + σ gives | Nominative | Dative plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| κ, γ, χ (velar) | ξ | φύλαξ | φύλαξι(ν) |
| π, β, φ (labial) | ψ | φλέψ | φλεψί(ν) |

Everywhere else the stop stands unchanged before a vowel: φύλακος, φύλακι, φύλακα.

## Common vocabulary

| Noun | Genitive | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| φύλαξ (m.) | φύλακος | guard |
| κῆρυξ (m.) | κήρυκος | herald |
| σάλπιγξ (f.) | σάλπιγγος | trumpet |
| αἴξ (m./f.) | αἰγός | goat |
| θρίξ (f.) | τριχός | hair |
| φλέψ (f.) | φλεβός | vein |
| γυνή (f.) | γυναικός | woman |

## Points to watch

- **The genitive is the only safe guide.** A nominative in -ξ can hide κ, γ or χ (φύλακος, σάλπιγγος, τριχός), and one in -ψ can hide π, β or φ. The nominative shows you the class; only the genitive shows you the stem.
- **θρίξ, τριχός is not two words.** The stem is θριχ-, but Greek avoids two aspirated consonants in neighbouring syllables, so the θ gives way to τ wherever the χ survives: τριχός, τριχί, τρίχες. In the nominative and dative plural the χ has already fused into ξ, the second aspirate is gone, and θ returns: θρίξ, θριξί(ν).
- **γυνή** is irregular in the nominative and in the vocative (ὦ γύναι); everything else is built regularly on the velar stem γυναικ-: γυναικός, γυναικί, γυναῖκα; pl. γυναῖκες, γυναικῶν, γυναιξί(ν), γυναῖκας.
- Monosyllabic stems accent the ending in the genitive and dative, singular and plural: φλεβός, φλεβί, φλεβῶν, φλεψί(ν).
- The vocative is the same as the nominative throughout this group.
- Neuters do not occur here: a Greek word cannot end in a stop, so a bare neuter stem in π or κ has no possible nominative.

## Historical Development

**The letters ξ and ψ are themselves a historical accident.** They exist to write exactly the clusters this lesson is about, and the Greek cities did not agree for a long time on whether to use them. Athens, in its own older alphabet, wrote these sounds out as two letters: **ΧΣ** for ξ and **ΦΣ** for ψ. Only with the official adoption of the Ionic alphabet in 403/402 BCE did the Athenian public inscriptions switch to the single letters. So a fifth-century Attic stone may spell φύλαξ as ΦΥΛΑΧΣ, and editions of inscriptions preserve this; the language is identical, only the spelling convention has changed.

**Homeric epic** adds the dative plural in **-εσσι(ν)** beside the inherited -σι(ν), an Aeolic form that epic spread right across the third declension. It gives a poet an extra syllable and avoids the heavy clusters that -σι produces — one of the clearest signs that the language of the epics was assembled for the convenience of verse rather than spoken anywhere as it stands.

**In Koine** the group is stable and conservative; no new sound changes touch it. What does change is the vocabulary rather than the paradigm: κῆρυξ "herald" and the verb κηρύσσω "proclaim" become central religious terms, and σάλπιγξ "trumpet" acquires an apocalyptic weight it never had in Attic prose.

## Examples

- οἱ φύλακες τὴν πόλιν φυλάττουσιν — "the guards guard the city"
- ὁ κῆρυξ τὸν ἀγῶνα ἤγγειλεν — "the herald announced the contest"
- διὰ τῶν φλεβῶν — "through the veins"

# Deponent Verbs

---

## 1. What a Deponent Verb Is

A **deponent** verb has an active **meaning** but only middle (or middle and passive) **forms**. It has no active present, and conjugates with the middle/passive endings throughout its present system, yet it is translated actively.

| Verb | Form | Meaning |
|------|------|---------|
| λύομαι (from λύω) | middle/passive of an active verb | "I am loosed" / "I loose for myself" |
| **ἔρχομαι** | deponent — no active exists | simply "I come" |

> **A note on the word.** *Deponent* is borrowed from Latin grammar, from *dēpōnere* "to lay aside": the image is of a verb that has laid its active forms aside. This book keeps the term, because it is the one Smyth and most textbooks use and the one you will meet everywhere else. The *Cambridge Grammar of Classical Greek* does **not** use it. It splits the class in two instead, according to what the aorist and future look like:
>
> * **middle-only verbs** (*media tantum*) — middle forms in the aorist and future: ἡγέομαι (aor. ἡγησάμην), κτάομαι (ἐκτησάμην), μηχανάομαι (ἐμηχανησάμην);
> * **passive-only verbs** (*passiva tantum*) — passive forms in the aorist and future: βούλομαι (aor. ἐβουλήθην), δύναμαι (ἐδυνήθην).
>
> Its reason for keeping the two apart is that they tend to express different kinds of meaning. That is exactly the division this lesson draws in §3.1 and §3.2, under the names *middle deponent* and *passive deponent* — so when you meet "media tantum" or "middle-only" in a reference grammar, these are the verbs it means.

Deponency is a property of the **word**, not of a tense or an ending. There is no special "deponent conjugation" to learn: deponents reuse exactly the middle/passive paradigms you meet in the ordinary voice lessons of this book. What this lesson teaches is how to **recognize** these verbs and how to **translate** them.

**The dictionary is the test:** if the headword ends in **‑μαι** and no active form of the verb exists beside it (βούλομαι, γίγνομαι, δύναμαι), the verb is deponent. If an active headword in ‑ω or ‑μι exists (λύω, δίδωμι, and see rule 5 below), a ‑μαι form you meet in a text is a true middle or passive, not a deponent.

## 2. Common Deponents Worth Knowing

| Verb | Meaning | Note |
|------|------|------|
| **ἔρχομαι** | to come, go | suppletive active aorist ἦλθον (§3.4) |
| **γίγνομαι** | to become, happen | aorist ἐγενόμην; perfect γέγονα is active in form |
| **βούλομαι** | to want, wish | passive deponent: aorist ἐβουλήθην (§3.2) |
| **δύναμαι** | to be able | athematic (§3.3); aorist ἐδυνήθην |
| **δέχομαι** | to receive, accept | middle deponent: aorist ἐδεξάμην |
| **μάχομαι** | to fight | takes the dative; aorist ἐμαχεσάμην |
| **ἀποκρίνομαι** | to answer | takes the dative |
| **αἰσθάνομαι** | to perceive | takes the genitive of the person, genitive or accusative of the thing; aorist ᾐσθόμην |
| **ἡγέομαι** | to lead; to consider | ε‑contract, conjugates like the middle of ποιέω |
| **οἴομαι** | to think | passive deponent: aorist ᾠήθην |
| **ἐνθυμέομαι** | to reflect on, consider | ε‑contract passive deponent: aorist ἐνεθυμήθην |
| **ἐπίσταμαι** | to know, understand | athematic (§3.3) |

## 3. The Four Situations to Recognize

### 3.1 Middle deponents
Their aorist is **middle** in form: ἐδεξάμην "I received," ἐμαχεσάμην "I fought," ἐγενόμην "I became." Conjugation: exactly the aorist middle paradigms of this book.

### 3.2 Passive deponents
Their aorist is **passive** in form (the θη‑aorist), still with active meaning: ἐβουλήθην "I wanted," ᾠήθην "I thought," ἐνεθυμήθην "I considered." Most passive deponents express mental action of some sort. When you meet a θη‑form of one of these verbs in the aorist‑passive lessons, translate it actively.

### 3.3 Athematic deponents
δύναμαι, ἐπίσταμαι, κεῖμαι, κάθημαι attach the middle endings **directly to the stem**, without a thematic vowel: δύνα‑μαι, δύνα‑σαι, δύνα‑ται, δυνά‑μεθα, δύνα‑σθε, δύνα‑νται. They belong with the **‑μι verbs**, and this book's (mi) lessons conjugate them.

### 3.4 Suppletive active tenses
Some verbs that are otherwise deponent do show **active‑form** tenses outside the present, especially in the aorist and perfect: ἔρχομαι has aorist **ἦλθον** and perfect **ἐλήλυθα**; γίγνομαι has perfect **γέγονα**. They are deponent in the sense that matters — no active present, active meaning throughout. These forms conjugate like ordinary active aorists and perfects — only the dictionary form tells you they belong to a deponent verb.

## 4. Key Rules & Common Pitfalls

| Rule | Explanation | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **1. Translate actively** | The form is middle/passive; the meaning is not. | βούλεται "he wants," not "he is wanted" |
| **2. The headword decides** | A ‑μαι headword with no active beside it marks a deponent. If an active form of the verb exists, the ‑μαι form is a middle, however it is translated. | λύομαι is *not* deponent (headword λύω) |
| **3. Learn the principal parts** | The aorist may be middle (ἐγενόμην), passive (ἐβουλήθην), or active suppletive (ἦλθον). | see §3 |
| **4. Don't confuse with meaningful middles** | Some active verbs have a middle with its own meaning — that is not deponency. | αἱρέω "I take" / αἱρέομαι "I choose"; πείθω "I persuade" / πείθομαι "I obey" |
| **5. Don't confuse with verbs that have a causative active** | Several ‑ομαι verbs look deponent and even take a θη‑aorist with active sense, but an active form of the verb does exist, with a causative meaning. They are middles, not deponents. | πορεύω "I make go, convey" / πορεύομαι "I travel" (ἐπορεύθην); φοβέω "I terrify" / φοβέομαι "I am afraid" (ἐφοβήθην) |
| **6. Middle futures of active verbs** | Several common active verbs have a future that is middle in form only ("semi‑deponents"). | ἀκούω → ἀκούσομαι, λαμβάνω → λήψομαι, ὁράω → ὄψομαι, βαίνω → βήσομαι |
| **7. Agent phrases are absent** | So long as a deponent carries its active meaning it is not a true passive, and no ὑπό + genitive agent accompanies it. (A few middle-only verbs do also form passive forms with passive meaning — ἐκτήθην "was acquired," ᾐτιάθην "was accused" — and those can take an agent.) | γίγνεται ταῦτα "these things happen" |

## 5. Example Sentences

| Greek | Translation |
|----------------------------|-------------|
| **ἔρχομαι εἰς τὴν πόλιν.** | I am coming into the city. |
| **οἱ στρατιῶται μάχονται τοῖς πολεμίοις.** | The soldiers fight the enemy. |
| **ἐβουλήθη ὁ βασιλεὺς ἀκοῦσαι.** | The king wanted to listen. (passive form, active meaning) |
| **οὐ δύναμαι λέγειν.** | I am not able to speak. (athematic) |
| **ἦλθον οἱ ξένοι εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν.** | The strangers came into the house. (suppletive active aorist of ἔρχομαι) |
| **ὁ διδάσκαλος φιλεῖται ὑπὸ τῶν μαθητῶν.** | The teacher is loved by the students. (true passive, for contrast) |

## 6. Usage & Context

1. **Deponents are everywhere.** Verbs of motion, mental state, perception, and speech are disproportionately deponent (ἔρχομαι, βούλομαι, αἰσθάνομαι, ἀποκρίνομαι); in most Greek prose a large share of the middle‑looking forms you meet are deponents. Trust the meaning, not the shape.
2. **In this book** deponent forms appear inside the regular voice lessons, since their endings are the regular middle/passive endings; the answer keys flag each one as *deponent — active in meaning* so you are never left guessing.

## 7. Historical Development

Deponency looks like an irregularity, but it is better understood as a **leftover from an older system**.

**Greek started with two voices, not three.** The oldest layer of the language opposes an active to a **middle**, with no passive. The Greek passive is a later, local invention: the θη‑aorist that marks it began as an *intransitive* or *stative* formation ("become X", "be in a state of X"), and only gradually specialized into a true passive. In the present, imperfect, perfect and pluperfect Greek never built a separate passive at all — which is exactly why those tenses have a single middle/passive form, as the voice lessons of this book keep noting.

Seen that way, deponents are not defective. They are verbs that stayed in the older two-voice system: they never needed an active, because the middle was always the right voice for what they mean. Verbs of motion (ἔρχομαι), change of state (γίγνομαι), perception (αἰσθάνομαι), mental state (βούλομαι, οἴομαι, ἐνθυμέομαι) and speech directed at someone (ἀποκρίνομαι) all describe the subject as personally involved — which is precisely what the middle is for. The traditional image of a verb "laying aside" its active is misleading; nothing was laid aside.

**Koine shifts deponents toward passive forms.** The θη‑aorist keeps spreading at the expense of the middle aorist, so a verb whose Attic aorist is middle may show a θη‑form in Koine:

| Verb | Attic aorist | Koine / NT aorist |
|---|---|---|
| ἀποκρίνομαι "answer" | ἀπεκρίνατο (middle) | **ἀπεκρίθη** (passive form) |
| γίγνομαι "become" | ἐγένετο (middle) | ἐγένετο, but also **ἐγενήθην** |

The meaning does not change; only the form does. So in a Koine text a θη‑ending is weaker evidence of a true passive than it would be in Plato — check the lexicon before translating one as a passive.

## 8. Quick Reference

| Situation | Recognize by | Example | Translate |
|-----------|--------------|---------|-----------|
| Middle deponent | headword ‑μαι, middle aorist | δέχομαι, ἐδεξάμην | actively |
| Passive deponent | headword ‑μαι, θη‑aorist | βούλομαι, ἐβουλήθην | actively |
| Athematic deponent | headword ‑μαι, no thematic vowel | δύναμαι, δύνασαι | actively |
| Suppletive active tense | active form, ‑μαι headword | ἦλθον ← ἔρχομαι | actively |
| Meaningful middle (not deponent) | headword ‑ω exists | αἱρέομαι ← αἱρέω | by its middle sense |
| Causative active exists (not deponent) | active ‑ω with causative sense | πορεύομαι ← πορεύω | actively, but it is a middle |
| Semi‑deponent future | active verb, middle future | ἀκούσομαι ← ἀκούω | actively |

# Didakta: A Grammar to Keep Beside This Book

---

## 1. Why This Book Needs One

You have now met four parts of the nominal system, and the pattern of this book should be clear: each lesson takes one cell of the grammar — a case, a declension, a tense, a mood — and teaches the forms that fill it, in the order your own texts make them worth learning.

That works because Greek marks so much **on the word**. But a great deal of what a sentence is doing is not marked on any word. Whether a clause states a purpose, a result, a condition or a fear; whether an infinitive is the object of a verb or the subject of the sentence; whether a participle is doing the work of an English relative clause or an English "because" — none of these has an ending of its own. They live in the relation between words, and a syllabus built out of endings has no cell for them.

This book handles that in two ways. A few constructions common enough to be taught in sequence get their own lessons, and a reference section at the back sets out the main clause types and shows what your chosen texts attest of each. Both are deliberately narrow. For everything else, you want a grammar.

---

## 2. What Didakta Is

**Didakta** is a grammar reference for Ancient Greek, written to cover exactly the ground that treebanks — and therefore this book's syllabus — do not reach. It describes itself as *a localizable grammar reference for the ancient Greek language, designed as a pedagogical resource for annotating syntactic features not covered in treebanks*.

Three things make it a good fit beside a Didaskalos textbook.

**It is modular.** Sections are short and self-contained, so you can read the one you need without working through what comes before it. That suits a book whose own order is decided by your corpus rather than by a fixed progression.

**It is built on the standard grammars.** It follows H. W. Smyth's *Greek Grammar for Colleges* (1920), the grammar this book's **Further reading** lines cite, while taking account of more recent scholarship including the *Cambridge Grammar of Classical Greek*. So a section number you find in Didakta will lead you to the fuller treatment in Smyth if you want it.

**It exists in several languages.** English, Persian and Kurdish, with Portuguese maintained separately — which matters for a book that is itself translated, and for readers who would rather not meet Greek syntax through a third language.

It is available as markdown, HTML, EPUB, PDF and Word, and it is archived with a permanent identifier:

- English: <https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.11216456>
- Repository, all languages and formats: <https://github.com/farnoosh-shamsian/Didakta>

Didakta is released under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial licence.

## 3. How to Use It Alongside This Book

**Look things up; do not read it through.** Didakta is a reference, not a course. Nothing in it is a prerequisite for the next lesson here, and you are not behind if you never open it.

**Go to it when a sentence resists you and the forms are not the problem.** The most useful signal is this: you have parsed every word in an exercise sentence correctly and still cannot say what the sentence means. That is almost always a syntactic question rather than a morphological one, and it is the question Didakta is written to answer.

**Use it for the terminology.** The grammatical vocabulary in this book — protasis, apodosis, attributive, predicative, indirect statement — is the standard vocabulary of Greek grammar, and Didakta defines it consistently across all its languages. If a term in a lesson here is doing more work than you can follow, that is a good place to look it up.

**The two are independent.** This book and Didakta are separate projects by the same author, and neither is generated from the other. Nothing here depends on your having Didakta open, and this section is a recommendation rather than an instruction — whether reading a syntax reference alongside a frequency-ordered course actually helps is not something anyone has measured.

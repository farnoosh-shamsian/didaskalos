# Didakta: A Grammar to Keep Beside This Book

---

## 1. Why This Book Needs One

You have now met four parts of the nominal system, and the pattern of this book should be clear: each lesson takes one cell of the grammar — a case, a declension, a tense, a mood — and teaches the forms that fill it, in the order your own texts make them worth learning.

That works because Greek marks so much **on the word**. But a great deal of what a sentence is doing is not marked on any word. Whether a clause states a purpose, a result, a condition or a fear; whether an infinitive is the object of a verb or the subject of the sentence; whether a dative names the instrument an action was done with or the place it was done in — none of these has an ending of its own. They live in the relation between words, and a syllabus built out of endings has no cell for them.

This book handles that in two ways. A few constructions common enough to be taught in sequence get their own lessons, and a reference section at the back sets out the main clause types and shows what your chosen texts attest of each. Both are deliberately narrow. For everything else, you want a grammar.

---

## 2. What Didakta Is

**Didakta Grammar for Annotation** describes itself as *a localizable grammar reference for the ancient Greek language, designed as a pedagogical resource for annotating syntactic features not covered in treebanks*.

It was written by Farnoosh Shamsian at Leipzig University, as part of doctoral work on teaching Ancient Greek to Persian speakers from annotated texts rather than from a textbook. The problem it was built to solve is the one this book has: a treebank will tell you that a noun is dative and that it modifies a verb, and stop there. It will not tell you whether the dative names an instrument — "the hero was struck **with a spear**" — or a place — "the hero was struck **on the shoulder**." Didakta supplies that second layer, and gives each use a name and a tag.

It was made mainly by rearranging and reformulating H. W. Smyth's *A Greek Grammar for Colleges* (1920), on the model of Jeffrey A. Rydberg-Cox's "Overview of Greek Syntax" (2000), with sections revised against the *Cambridge Grammar of Classical Greek* (Cambridge, 2018) and the Pedalion grammar.

Three properties matter for how you use it.

**It is modular.** Each entry stands on its own and barely refers to the others, so you can read the one you need without working through what comes before it, and the order of the sections is not load-bearing. That suits a book whose own order is decided by your corpus rather than by a fixed progression.

**It explains Greek without comparing it to anything.** Most English grammars of Greek quietly lean on English — they explain the Greek article briefly because the reader already has one, and describe a use of the imperfect as "where we might expect the aorist," which is only true of a reader whose first language works like English. Didakta strips those comparisons out and describes Greek on its own terms, including the parts an English speaker would find obvious. That is what makes it translatable; it also means nothing in it assumes a particular first language of yours.

**Its tags are the same in every language.** Each entry carries a short identifier — `§DatInstr`, `§AorGnom`, `§PartSupp` — and the identifiers do not change between versions. A passage tagged by an English-speaking annotator can therefore be read by a Persian or Kurdish speaker in their own language. English, Persian and Kurdish are available, with a Brazilian Portuguese version maintained separately.

There are 129 tagged entries in twenty-four sections.

## 3. What an Entry Looks Like

This is the whole of §DatInstr, the dative of instrument:

> **§DatInstr. Instrument**
>
> The dative of instrument expresses the instrument or tool by which or with which an action is done or accompanied. This use of the dative is the representative of the lost instrumental case.
>
> → "ἔβαλλέ με **λίθοις**" **L. 3.8**; ⇾ he hit me with stone
>
> → "ἵησι **τῇ ἀξίνῃ**" **X. A. 1.5.12**; ⇾ he hurls his ax at him (hurls with his ax)
>
> → "φυλαττόμενοι **φύλαξι**" **X. A. 6.4.27**; ⇾ defending themselves by pickets
>
> *Smyth 1503–1511, Pedalion 216, CGCG 30.43*

Every entry has the same four parts: the tag and title, a short description, real examples with their citations and translations, and a footnote pointing to the fuller treatment in Smyth, Pedalion and the *Cambridge Grammar*. So an entry is both an answer and a place to start looking if the answer is too short.

## 5. Four Places It Does the Most Work

**The cases.** This is the part that speaks most directly to the lessons you have just finished. Those lessons teach you to produce and recognize the endings; Didakta's forty-seven case entries name the jobs those endings do. When you can see that a word is genitive but not why it is genitive, you are looking for one of seventeen entries, and the context of the sentence picks it.

**Tense outside the indicative.** The present, future, aorist and perfect sections each end with a "non-indicative" entry making the same point: in the subjunctive, optative, imperative, infinitive and participle, the tense of a Greek verb is normally **aspect and not time**. An aorist infinitive is not a past infinitive. This book teaches those forms tense by tense and mood by mood, so the point comes up constantly, and it is worth reading once in full.

**The middle voice.** Seven entries against one each for active and passive, because "middle" is not one thing. A middle verb may act on itself, act for itself, speak, move its own body, think or feel, act reciprocally with someone else, or simply undergo a change with no agent in view. This book's lesson on deponent verbs covers the words that have no active forms at all; Didakta covers what the voice is doing when there is a choice.

**The infinitive.** Five entries, and the first division is the one to learn. A **dynamic** infinitive names a potential action, and completes verbs of wanting, being able, commanding, teaching, trying — its negative is μή, and its tense is purely aspectual. A **declarative** infinitive reports a statement or belief in indirect discourse, after φημί, λέγω, νομίζω, δοκέω — its negative is οὐ, and its tense keeps the tense of the words being reported. The other three entries cover the parenthetical idioms, the infinitive standing in for an imperative, and the **articular** infinitive, where a neuter article turns the verb into a noun phrase that can take any case while still governing its own object. So when you have parsed a form as "aorist infinitive active" and are still stuck, the question you actually have is which of these five it is, and that question has an entry.

## 6. How to Use It Alongside This Book

**Look things up; do not read it through.** Didakta is a reference, not a course. Nothing in it is a prerequisite for the next lesson here, and you are not behind if you never open it.

**Go to it when a sentence resists you and the forms are not the problem.** The most useful signal is this: you have parsed every word in an exercise sentence correctly and still cannot say what the sentence means. That is almost always a syntactic question rather than a morphological one, and it is the question Didakta is written to answer.

**Work from the form to the section.** Your lesson here tells you what a form *is*; the matching section there lists what that form can *do*. Parse first, then read the list of entries for that case or tense or mood, then let the rest of the sentence choose between them. The order matters, because most of these distinctions cannot be made from the word in isolation.

**Use the tags as search keys.** The identifiers are stable, so they can be attached to words in a text and searched later. Book one of the *Iliad* has been published this way — the Greek, a word-aligned Persian and Kurdish translation, the treebank and Didakta tags in one dataset — so that searching `DatLoc` returns every dative of place in the book, each in its context and with its translation. That is a different way of learning a construction from reading a paragraph about it: you meet twenty instances and see what they have in common.

**Use it for the terminology.** The grammatical vocabulary in this book — protasis, apodosis, attributive, predicative, indirect statement — is the standard vocabulary of Greek grammar, and Didakta defines it consistently across all its languages. If a term in a lesson here is doing more work than you can follow, that is a good place to look it up.

**The two are independent.** This book and Didakta are separate projects by the same author, and neither is generated from the other. Nothing here depends on your having Didakta open, and this section is a recommendation rather than an instruction — whether reading a syntax reference alongside a frequency-ordered course actually helps is not something anyone has measured.

# Didakta: A Syntax Reference to Keep Beside This Book

---

## 1. What the Endings Do Not Tell You

Each lesson in this book takes one cell of the grammar — a case, a declension, a tense, a mood — and teaches the forms that fill it. That works because Greek marks so much **on the word itself**.

But much of what a sentence is doing is marked on no word at all. Whether a clause states a purpose, a result, a condition or a fear; whether an infinitive is the object of a verb or the subject of the sentence; whether a dative names the instrument an action was done with or the place it was done in — none of this has an ending of its own. It lives in the relation between words, and a syllabus built out of endings has no cell for it.

This book covers a little of that ground. A few constructions common enough to teach in sequence have their own lessons, and the reference section at the back sets out the main clause types. For everything else you want a syntax reference.

---

## 2. What It Is

*Didakta Grammar for Annotation* takes each form you have learned to recognize and lists the jobs that form can do. Parsing a word — or reading it off a treebank — tells you that a noun is dative and that it belongs with a particular verb, and stops there. It does not tell you whether that dative names an instrument, "the hero was struck **with a spear**", or a place, "the hero was struck **on the shoulder**". Didakta names the second layer: **127 grammatical entries in twenty-three sections**, one for each use, each with a short identifier of its own.

Two things about it matter for how you use it.

**Every entry stands alone.** Nothing in an entry assumes you have read the entries before it, so you can open the book at the one you need. Its order is not load-bearing, which suits a course whose own order is set by your corpus.

**The identifiers do not change between languages.** `§DatInstr`, `§AorGnom`, `§PartSupp` are the same tags in the English, the Persian and the Brazilian Portuguese version. So a reference you meet in one language can be followed up in another, and a passage tagged by one reader can be read by another in their own.

## 3. What an Entry Looks Like

This is the whole of §DatInstr, the dative of instrument:

> **§DatInstr. Instrument**
>
> The dative of instrument expresses the instrument or tool by which or with which an action is done or accompanied. This use of the dative is the representative of the lost instrumental case.
>
> → "ἔβαλλέ με **λίθοις**" **L. 3.8**; ⇾ he hit me with stone
>
> → "ἵησι **τῇ ἀξίνῃ**" **X. A. 1.5.12**; ⇾ he hurls his ax at him (hurls with his ax)
>
> → "φυλαττόμενοι **φύλαξι**" **X. A. 6.4.27**; ⇾ defending themselves by pickets
>
> *Smyth 1503–1511, Pedalion 216, CGCG 30.43*

Every entry has these four parts: the tag and title, a short description, real examples with citations and translations, and a footnote pointing to the fuller treatment in Smyth, Pedalion and the *Cambridge Grammar*. An entry is therefore both an answer and a place to start looking when the answer is too short.

## 5. Where You Will Use It Most

**The cases.** The lessons you have just finished teach you to build and recognize the endings; the forty-seven case entries name the jobs those endings do. When you can see that a word is genitive but not why it is genitive, you are choosing among seventeen entries, and the rest of the sentence decides which.

**Tense outside the indicative.** The present, future, aorist and perfect sections each close with a "non-indicative" entry making the same point: in the subjunctive, optative, imperative, infinitive and participle, the tense of a Greek verb is normally **aspect and not time**. An aorist infinitive is not a past infinitive. This book teaches those forms tense by tense and mood by mood, so the point comes up constantly and is worth reading once in full.

**The middle voice.** Seven entries, against one each for active and passive, because "middle" is not one thing. A middle verb may act on itself, act for itself, speak, move its own body, think or feel, act reciprocally with someone else, or simply undergo a change with no agent in view. The lesson on deponent verbs here covers the verbs that have no active forms at all; Didakta covers what the voice is doing when there is a choice.

**The infinitive.** Five entries, and the first division is the one to learn. A **dynamic** infinitive names a potential action and completes verbs of wanting, being able, commanding, teaching and trying: its negative is μή, and its tense is purely aspectual. A **declarative** infinitive reports a statement or belief after φημί, λέγω, νομίζω, δοκέω: its negative is οὐ, and its tense is that of the words being reported. The other three cover the parenthetical idioms, the infinitive standing in for an imperative, and the **articular** infinitive, where a neuter article turns the verb into a noun phrase that can take any case while still governing its own object. So when you have parsed a form as "aorist infinitive active" and are still stuck, the question you actually have is which of the five it is.

## 6. How to Use It

**Look things up; do not read it through.** It is a reference, not a course. Nothing in it is a prerequisite for the next lesson here, and you are not behind if you never open it.

**Go to it when a sentence resists you and the forms are not the problem.** If you have parsed every word in an exercise sentence correctly and still cannot say what the sentence means, the question is syntactic rather than morphological, and that is the question Didakta answers.

**Work from the form to the section.** The lesson here tells you what a form *is*; the matching section there lists what that form can *do*. Parse first, read the entries for that case or tense or mood, then let the rest of the sentence choose between them. The order matters, because most of these distinctions cannot be made from a word in isolation.

**Use it for the terminology.** Protasis, apodosis, attributive, predicative, indirect statement — the grammatical vocabulary of these lessons is the standard vocabulary of Greek grammar, and Didakta defines it the same way in each of its languages.

**Use the tags as search keys.** Because the identifiers are stable, they can be attached to the words of a text and searched afterwards. Book one of the *Iliad* has been published this way, so that searching `DatLoc` returns every dative of place in the book, each in its context and with a translation. Meeting twenty instances of a construction is a different way of learning it from reading a paragraph about it.

**Treat this as a suggestion, not an instruction.** This book and Didakta are separate projects, and neither is generated from the other. Nothing here depends on your having Didakta open, and whether reading a syntax reference alongside a frequency-ordered course actually helps is not something anyone has measured.

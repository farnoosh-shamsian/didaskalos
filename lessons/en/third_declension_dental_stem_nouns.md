# Third Declension Dental Stem Nouns (-ς / -μα)

These nouns attach the shared third declension endings to a stem ending in a **dental**: τ, δ or θ. Unlike the labials and velars, a dental leaves no trace when it meets σ — it simply drops out:

- ἐλπιδ- + ς → **ἐλπίς**, ἐλπίδ-ος "hope" (stem ἐλπιδ-)
- σωματ- → **σῶμα**, σώματ-ος "body" (stem σωματ-)

This is the largest and most frequent group in the third declension, and it is also the one where the nominative hides the most. ἐλπίς gives no hint that a δ is coming; σῶμα gives no hint of a τ. The genitive singular is the form to learn.

Gender is not predictable from the endings, with one large exception noted below; learn it with the article.

## The shared endings

| Case | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | -ς, or bare stem (neuter) | -ες (neuter -α) |
| Genitive | -ος | -ων |
| Dative | -ι | -σι(ν) |
| Accusative | -α (neuter = nom.) | -ας (neuter -α) |
| Vocative | usually = nom. or bare stem | -ες (neuter -α) |

## Paradigms

ἐλπίς "hope" (feminine, stem ἐλπιδ-) and σῶμα "body" (neuter, stem σωματ-):

| Case | Singular | Plural | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | ἐλπίς | ἐλπίδες | σῶμα | σώματα |
| Genitive | ἐλπίδος | ἐλπίδων | σώματος | σωμάτων |
| Dative | ἐλπίδι | ἐλπίσι(ν) | σώματι | σώμασι(ν) |
| Accusative | ἐλπίδα | ἐλπίδας | σῶμα | σώματα |
| Vocative | ἐλπί | ἐλπίδες | σῶμα | σώματα |

## Sound changes before -σ

The dental disappears before σ, in the nominative singular and the dative plural alike:

| Stem ends in | + σ gives | Nominative | Dative plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| τ, δ, θ (dental) | σ (dental drops) | ἐλπίς | ἐλπίσι(ν) |
| ντ | σ (with the ν lost too) | γίγας | γίγασι(ν) |

A neuter stem has no -ς in the nominative at all. It would end in the bare stem, but a Greek word cannot end in τ, so the τ simply falls away: σωματ- → σῶμα, ὀνοματ- → ὄνομα.

| Noun | Genitive | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ὄνομα (n.) | ὀνόματος | name |
| πρᾶγμα (n.) | πράγματος | thing, affair |
| χρῆμα (n.) | χρήματος | thing; (pl.) money |
| σῶμα (n.) | σώματος | body |
| νύξ (f.) | νυκτός | night |
| παῖς (m./f.) | παιδός | child |
| πούς (m.) | ποδός | foot |
| ἐλπίς (f.) | ἐλπίδος | hope |
| χάρις (f.) | χάριτος | grace, favour |
| πατρίς (f.) | πατρίδος | fatherland |

## Points to watch

- **The neuters in -μα, -ματος are the class to know.** ὄνομα, πρᾶγμα, χρῆμα, σῶμα and hundreds more: the ending -μα is a reliable sign of a neuter dental stem, and recognizing it pays off on almost every page.
- The neuter rule from the second declension holds here too: nominative = accusative = vocative, and the plural of all three is -α (σώματα).
- **νύξ looks like a velar stem and is not.** The stem is νυκτ-; in the nominative the κτ meets ς and is written ξ, but the genitive νυκτός shows the dental, and the dative plural is νυξί(ν).
- Some dental stems take -ν in the accusative singular instead of -α: χάριν from χάρις, ἔριν from ἔρις.
- Monosyllabic stems accent the ending in the genitive and dative: νυκτός, νυκτί, νυκτῶν, νυξί(ν); ποδός, ποδί, ποσί(ν). παῖς follows this in the singular (παιδός, παιδί) but keeps παίδων in the genitive plural.
- The vocative singular of the feminines in -ις is the bare stem without its dental: ἐλπί, πατρί.

## Historical Development

**Homer's dative plural is the tell.** Beside the -σι(ν) you have learned, epic has a second dative plural in **-εσσι(ν)**, taken into the epic language from Aeolic, and the dental stems supply its most familiar example:

| Attic | Homeric |
|---|---|
| ποσί(ν) "to the feet" | **πόδεσσι(ν)** |
| παισί(ν) "to the children" | **παίδεσσι(ν)** |

The advantage to a poet is obvious: -εσσι adds a syllable and sidesteps the awkward clusters that -σι produces after a dental. It is one of the clearest cases of Homeric Greek being a made language, assembled from several dialects for the convenience of verse.

Two footnotes to that. The ending is characteristic of the Aeolic dialects — Lesbian, Thessalian, Boeotian — but not confined to them: the inscriptions show it in early Delphian, East Locrian, Elean and in several Corinthian colonies too. And it was not invented for poetry. It began as the σ-stem dative (γένεσσι, where the ε belongs to the stem) spreading by analogy to consonant stems where it had no business being. The poets found it convenient; they did not make it.

**The -μα, -ματος class expands enormously in Koine.** Neuter dental stems were always numerous, but in Hellenistic Greek they became the standard way to coin a noun meaning "the result of doing X", and the New Testament vocabulary is full of them: **πνεῦμα** "spirit", **ῥῆμα** "word", **θέλημα** "will", **βάπτισμα** "baptism", **χάρισμα** "gift", **κρίμα** "judgement", **αἷμα** "blood", beside ὄνομα and σῶμα. If you are reading Koine, no other noun pattern repays the effort as quickly.

**In Koine** the declension is otherwise conservative, though the pressure that eventually dissolved it is already visible: a few third declension nouns begin to pick up first declension endings, a drift that only finishes long after our period.

## Examples

- τὰ πράγματα χαλεπά ἐστιν — "the affairs are difficult" (neuter plural with a singular verb)
- τῆς νυκτός — "by night" (genitive of time)
- ἐλπίδα ἔχομεν — "we have hope"
